\vspace{1em}

\begin{abstract}

    Local political institutions face a tradeoff between more local representation, which results in politicians who represent more concentrated subsets of people, and 
    more centralized decision-making, which allows decisions to be made quicker but at the expense of potentially valuable local knowledge.
    This paper studies this tradeoff in the setting of New York City in the aftermath of a US Supreme Court decision that resulted in land-use authority being transferred from a centralized board to 
    city council districts of a much finer geography. 
    Although the reform was designed to improve representation and participation in local politics, it may also have also strengthened local veto power in pockets of the city that were more opposed to new housing supply. 
    I find descriptive evidence that 2010 City Council districts with a higher baseline share of homeowners see a much lower share of aggregate housing supply added after the court-induced reform, 
    especially for larger buildings where local opposition and discretionary review are more likely to be relevant.
    These findings are preliminary, but they suggest that reforms increasing local representation may have unintentionally shifted housing production away from homeowner-heavy neighborhoods, which could have large implications for citywide housing affordability and spatial inequality.
\end{abstract}
