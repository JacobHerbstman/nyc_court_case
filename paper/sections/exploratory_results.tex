\section{Exploratory Results}
\label{sec:exploratory_results}

This section presents the first descriptive patterns in the evolution of housing development over time within the city.
The goal is to assess whether the raw data move in the direction the institutional story implies, and whether the pattern is concentrated on the housing margins where discretionary review should matter most.

\subsection{Raw Trends by Treatment Tercile}

Figure~\ref{fig:tercile_trends} groups community districts into low, middle, and high terciles of the within-borough homeownership treatment, then plots each group's share of borough-total housing production over time.
The comparison is made within borough before aggregating to the citywide figure.

The large-building panel is the main focus.
Before the reform, high-treatment districts account for a substantial share of 50+ unit construction.
After 2010, the ranking reverses. In 2010--2019, high-treatment districts account for 15 percent of 50+ unit construction, while low-treatment districts account for 43 percent.
In 2020--2025, those shares are 13 and 49 percent, respectively.
The total-units panel shows a similar, if somewhat less stark, pattern. The 1--4 unit margin, by contrast, does not exhibit the same gradient --- which is reassuring, since small buildings are less likely to be the margin where member deference operates.

\subsection{Brooklyn Case Study}

Brooklyn offers a useful case study because it contains a mix of neighborhood types within a single borough: low-homeownership districts with substantial recent redevelopment, high-homeownership districts with lower-density built form, and several neighborhoods where demand pressure and political resistance plausibly overlap.
That makes it a natural place to examine the mechanism before moving to a full citywide design.

The Brooklyn descriptive pattern is consistent with the tercile trends.
In 2020--2025, high-treatment Brooklyn districts average 111.0 total new-building units per 10,000 occupied units, compared with 187.3 in low-treatment districts.
For 50+ unit buildings specifically, the gap is 85.1 versus 135.5 units per 10,000 occupied units.
The project-count margin points in the same direction: high-treatment districts average 2.04 projects of 50+ units per CD-year, against 3.28 in low-treatment districts.

The small-building margin tells a different story.
High-treatment Brooklyn districts are slightly \textit{higher} on 1--2 unit new-building construction, 1.10 versus 0.84 per 10,000 occupied units.
This asymmetry suggests that the treatment is not simply capturing that homeowner-heavy places build less of everything.
The effect is concentrated on the large-building margins most likely to involve discretionary review, community opposition, and member deference.

\subsection{Event-Study Results}
\label{subsec:regression_analogues}

\input{../tasks/estimate_cd_homeownership_long_units_event_study/output/cd_homeownership_long_units_event_5_plus_four_controls_macros.tex}

I next estimate a CD-level regression analogue of Figure~\ref{fig:tercile_trends}.
The preferred outcome is units in 5+ unit new buildings per 10,000 occupied housing units in 1990 --- a margin broad enough to be well-powered, but one that still excludes the 1--4 unit range where discretionary review should matter less.
I plot the 1--4 unit series alongside as a placebo.

The event-study specification is
\begin{equation}
Y_{cbt}
= \alpha_c + \lambda_{b p(t)}
+ \sum_{p \neq p_0} \beta_p \left(T_c \times \mathbf{1}\{p(t)=p\}\right)
+ \sum_{p \neq p_0} \gamma_p' \left(X_c \times \mathbf{1}\{p(t)=p\}\right)
+ \varepsilon_{cbt},
\label{eq:homeownership_event_study}
\end{equation}
where \(c\) indexes community districts, \(b\) boroughs, and \(p(t)\) period bins.
The omitted period \(p_0\) is 1985--1989.
The treatment \(T_c\) is the 1990 homeownership share, standardized within borough.
The controls \(X_c\) are 1990 median household income, the 1990 poverty share, log 1990 occupied units, and pre-period production on the same outcome scale, each interacted with the period bins.
Fixed effects absorb permanent CD differences and borough-period housing cycles; standard errors are clustered by CD.

    \caption{Event-Study Gradients by Housing Margin}
    \label{fig:homeownership_event_study_5_plus}
    \figurenotes{Figure plots coefficients on within-borough standardized 1990 homeownership from Equation~\ref{eq:homeownership_event_study}. Outcomes are units in 1--4 unit and 5+ unit new buildings per 10,000 occupied housing units in 1990. The omitted period is 1985--1989. Controls are 1990 median household income, the 1990 poverty share, log 1990 occupied units, and pre-period production, each interacted with period bins. Standard errors are clustered by CD.}

Figure~\ref{fig:homeownership_event_study_5_plus} shows a delayed homeowner gradient rather than an immediate post-1990 break.
The 1980--1984 placebo coefficient is positive, \HomeownFivePlusEventEarlyEightiesCoef, relative to 1985--1989.
The 1990s and 2000s coefficients are also small, \HomeownFivePlusEventNinetiesCoef\ and \HomeownFivePlusEventTwoThousandsCoef.
The gradient turns sharply negative in the later periods: a one-standard-deviation increase in 1990 homeownership exposure is associated with \HomeownFivePlusEventTwentyTensCoef\ fewer 5+ unit new-building units per 10,000 baseline occupied units in 2010--2019 \((p=\HomeownFivePlusEventTwentyTensP)\), and \HomeownFivePlusEventTwentyTwentiesCoef\ fewer in 2020--2025 \((p=\HomeownFivePlusEventTwentyTwentiesP)\).
The 1--4 unit placebo shows no comparable pattern, with coefficients of \HomeownOneFourEventTwentyTensCoef\ in 2010--2019 and \HomeownOneFourEventTwentyTwentiesCoef\ in 2020--2025.

Table~\ref{tab:homeownership_long_units_long_difference} reports the corresponding long-difference estimates.
The results are consistent with the event study: the 1985--1989 versus 1980--1984 placebo is \HomeownFivePlusLongDiffPlaceboCoef\ \((p=\HomeownFivePlusLongDiffPlaceboP)\), while the 2010s and 2020s long differences are \HomeownFivePlusLongDiffTwentyTensCoef\ \((p=\HomeownFivePlusLongDiffTwentyTensP)\) and \HomeownFivePlusLongDiffTwentyTwentiesCoef\ \((p=\HomeownFivePlusLongDiffTwentyTwentiesP)\).
These estimates do not capture the total citywide effect of \textit{Morris}; they measure whether post-\textit{Morris} production shifted away from homeowner-heavy community districts within the same borough.
