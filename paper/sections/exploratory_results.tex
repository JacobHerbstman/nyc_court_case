\section{Exploratory Results}
\label{sec:exploratory_results}

This section shows the first descriptive patterns on the evolution of housing development over time within the city.
They are meant to check whether the raw data move in the direction implied by the institutional story, and whether the pattern is concentrated on the housing margins where discretionary review should matter most.

\subsection{Raw Trends by Treatment Tercile}

Figure~\ref{fig:tercile_trends} groups community districts into low, middle, and high terciles of the within-borough homeownership treatment. The figure then plots each group's share of borough-total housing production over time. This presentation keeps the comparison within borough before aggregating to the citywide figure.

The large-building panel is the main object of interest. Before the reform, high-treatment districts account for a large share of 50+ unit construction. After 2010, the ranking reverses. In 2010--2019, high-treatment districts account for 15 percent of 50+ unit construction, while low-treatment districts account for 43 percent. In 2020--2025, the high-treatment share is 13 percent and the low-treatment share is 49 percent. The total-units panel shows a similar, though less stark, pattern. By contrast, the 1--4 unit margin does not line up in the same way, which is useful because small buildings are less likely to be the margin where member deference bites.

\subsection{Brooklyn Case Study}

Brooklyn is a useful case study because it has a mix of neighborhood types within one borough. It contains low-homeownership districts with substantial recent redevelopment, high-homeownership districts with lower-density built form, and several places where demand pressure and political resistance plausibly overlap. That makes it a good place to look at mechanism before moving to a full citywide design.

The Brooklyn descriptive pattern is consistent with the tercile trends. In 2020--2025, high-treatment Brooklyn districts average 111.0 total new-building units per 10,000 occupied units, compared with 187.3 in low-treatment districts. For 50+ unit buildings, the gap is 85.1 versus 135.5 units per 10,000 occupied units. The project-count margin points the same way: high-treatment districts average 2.04 50+ unit projects per CD-year, compared with 3.28 in low-treatment districts.

The small-building margin is different. High-treatment Brooklyn districts are slightly higher on 1--2 unit new-building units per 10,000 occupied units, 1.10 versus 0.84. That contrast matters. It suggests that the homeowner gradient is not simply a statement that homeowner-heavy places build less of everything. It is stronger on the large-building margins that are more likely to involve discretionary review, community opposition, and Council member deference.

The next step is to connect these production patterns more directly to the discretionary pipeline. The most useful additions are ULURP applications and outcomes, community-board and borough-president recommendations, Council-stage votes, and a cleaner distinction between private development and public or HPD-led projects. Those data would help separate three channels that currently sit together in the raw construction series: fewer applications in homeowner-heavy districts, higher rejection or delay conditional on application, and lower build-out yield after approval.
