\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:empirical_strategy}

\subsection{Institutional Setting}

Before \textit{Morris}, final land-use review in New York City ran through the Board of Estimate. 
The Board combined three citywide officials, who together held six votes, with the five borough presidents, who held one vote each. 
The Supreme Court's opinion describes the Board as holding broad fiscal and legislative powers, including control over land use, franchises, contracts, and parts of the budget process \citep{board_of_estimate_of_city_of_new_york_v_morris_notitle_1989}.
The key is that key land-use decisions were previously made by a body whose smallest geographic representative was the borough, a large swath of the city, rather than a district-based councilmember who represents a much smaller and potentially less representative constituency

The 1989 Charter revision changed that allocation of authority. After the Board was abolished, final review of many land-use actions shifted to the City Council \citep{lane_policy_1998}. The Council is a 51-member districted legislature. A land-use application is still reviewed by the community board, borough president, and City Planning Commission, but the Council now sits at the final legislative stage for many discretionary approvals. The mayor can veto some Council actions, but the practical politics of district-specific land use are dominated by the Council.

The key informal institution is member deference. Under member deference, the Council generally follows the local councilmember on land-use applications in that member's district. The 2025 Charter Revision Commission describes this practice as outside the Charter but central to how land use works in practice \citep{new_york_city_charter_revision_commission_charter_2025}. This is the link between the court case and housing supply. The formal Charter reform gave the Council final review authority. Member deference then made that authority local.

\subsection{Unit of Analysis}

The empirical unit is the community district. 
Community districts are not the same as Council districts, and that distinction is useful. Council districts are the political units through which member deference operates, but they are redrawn over time and coverage of exact boundaries is spotty going back to the 1980's.
Community districts are stable neighborhood units that can be followed across the pre- and post-\textit{Morris} periods. 
Many other datasets also aggregate to the community-district level which makes it a convenient and consistent level in which to analyze housing flows.

This unit choice also keeps the treatment from being mechanically defined by post-reform political boundaries. 
The treatment is measured using 1990 community-district homeownership, just as the institutional change was taking effect. 
The outcomes then track housing production in the same community districts over time.

\subsection{Treatment Definition}

The treatment is baseline homeowner concentration relative to the borough. Let $H_{cb,1990}$ be the 1990 owner-occupied share of occupied housing units in community district $c$ in borough $b$, and let $H_{b,1990}$ be the corresponding borough-wide owner-occupied share. The raw treatment is:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta H_{cb,1990} = H_{cb,1990} - H_{b,1990}.
\end{equation}

The main measure standardizes this difference within borough:

\begin{equation}
    T_{cb} = \frac{\Delta H_{cb,1990} - \overline{\Delta H}_{b,1990}}{s_b(\Delta H_{cb,1990})}.
\end{equation}

This definition is deliberately within borough. Boroughs differ sharply in housing stock, density, transit access, ownership rates, and long-run development pressure. A citywide treatment would partly compare Staten Island to Manhattan or outer Queens to the Bronx. The within-borough measure instead asks whether homeowner-heavy districts within the same borough experienced different construction trajectories after land-use authority became more district based.

The identifying logic is simple. If member deference gives local councilmembers more effective control over discretionary land use, then places with stronger homeowner constituencies should have more political capacity to resist large buildings. That prediction should be strongest for multi-unit and large-building construction, where local opposition and discretionary review are most relevant. It should be weaker for small buildings, which are less likely to require the same political process.
