\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Real estate and rental prices in major U.S. metropolitan areas have risen sharply over recent decades, outpacing median income growth \cite{joint_center_for_housing_studies_of_harvard_university_americas_2024}.
New York City has long ranked among the most expensive cities in the country, and has grown more expensive still in recent years \cite{glaeser_why_2005-1}.
A substantial literature links these trends to land-use regulation --- and in particular to discretionary land-use decision-making --- as a central mechanism suppressing housing supply growth and driving up prices \cite{fischel_homevoter_2009,hilber_origins_2013, paciorek_supply_2013, khan_decentralized_2021,kulka_how_2022}.

This paper examines whether increased local control affects housing supply, using a sharp institutional change in New York City's land-use process.
Until 1989, the Uniform Land Use Review Procedure (ULURP) culminated in the Board of Estimate, a citywide body comprising the mayor, comptroller, city council president, and the five borough presidents.
In \textit{Board of Estimate of City of New York v. Morris}, the U.S. Supreme Court held that the Board's structure violated the one-person, one-vote principle, since boroughs with very different populations received equal representation through their borough presidents \cite{board_of_estimate_of_city_of_new_york_v_morris_notitle_1989}.
The 1989 Charter revision induced by the Morris decision substantially reorganized that authority.
After the Board was abolished, final review of many land-use actions shifted to the City Council \citep{lane_policy_1998}.
The Council is a 51-member districted legislature.
Applications continue to pass through the community board, borough president, and City Planning Commission, but the Council now sits at the final legislative stage for many discretionary approvals. While the mayor retains veto authority over some Council actions, the practical politics of district-specific land use are dominated by the Council itself.

Although the Charter vests formal land-use authority in the Council as a whole, the Council generally defers to the local member on district-specific applications \cite{donaldson_charter_2025}.
In practice, this gives each councilmember substantial control over projects in their district.
If homeowners are the constituency most likely to oppose large new buildings \cite{fischel_homevoter_2009}, then the shift from borough-scale to district-scale review should matter most in places where homeowners were especially concentrated relative to the rest of their borough.

I measure the likelihood of opposition using 1990 homeownership rates in New York City's community districts (CDs), which serve as a proxy for constituencies with the strongest incentives to resist new development, particularly of rental or affordable housing \cite{khan_decentralized_2021}.
Community districts are useful for this purpose because they are stable neighborhood units observed both before and after the reform, unlike city council districts. The 59 community districts are also similar in scale to the 51 council districts, making them a natural analytic unit.
The main treatment measure is a district's 1990 homeownership share minus its borough's 1990 homeownership share, standardized within borough, which isolates variation in local opposition without conflating borough-level differences in homeownership and development activity.
A high-homeownership district in Manhattan is not politically or physically comparable to one in Staten Island.
The treatment instead asks whether, within the same borough, homeowner-heavy community districts experienced different post-reform housing trajectories than their lower-homeownership neighbors.

I find suggestive evidence consistent with this hypothesis.
Raw trends show that after 2010, high-treatment districts account for a sharply smaller share of large-building construction than low-treatment districts.
In 2010--2019, the high-treatment tercile accounts for 15 percent of borough-total 50+ unit construction, compared with 43 percent in the low-treatment tercile. In 2020--2025, the corresponding shares are 13 percent and 49 percent.
The Brooklyn case study reinforces this pattern in a more concrete setting: high-treatment Brooklyn districts produce substantially fewer large-building units, while the small-building margin moves little or in the opposite direction.
