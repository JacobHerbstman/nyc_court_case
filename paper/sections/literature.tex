\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

This project builds on a large literature documenting that land-use regulation limits housing supply and raises costs. \citet{glaeser_why_2005} argue that regulation helps explain why Manhattan prices sit far above construction costs, and \citet{glaeser_economic_2018} frame this gap as an implicit regulatory tax. \citet{paciorek_supply_2013} shows that supply constraints shape not only price levels but also how quickly markets adjust to demand shocks. \citet{turner_land_2014} estimate the welfare costs of land-use regulation, while \citet{molloy_housing_2022} connect housing supply limits to affordability and household location. The common point is that housing supply is not just a technological margin. It is also a political and administrative margin.

A second strand asks why local governments restrict housing even when new supply would generate broad gains.
\citet{fischel_homevoter_2009} gives the canonical homeowner mechanism: owner-occupiers hold large, undiversified stakes in local property values and therefore have strong incentives to oppose changes they perceive as risky.
\citet{ortalo-magne_political_2014} formalize how homeownership and anti-growth politics can reinforce each other.
\citet{einstein_neighborhood_2019} show that participants in local land-use meetings are unrepresentative of the broader public,
and \citet{hankinson_when_2018} shows that support for housing varies sharply with spatial scale.
These papers motivate the treatment used here: relative homeownership within a borough is a proxy for the political concentration of residents with the strongest incentives to resist large buildings nearby.

A newer set of papers treats the scale of land-use authority as a direct determinant of housing outcomes. \citet{mast_warding_2024} finds that moving from at-large to ward elections reduces housing permits by shifting representation toward smaller constituencies.
\citet{khan_decentralized_2021} studies Chicago's aldermanic privilege and shows that local control can make zoning more restrictive near ward boundaries.
\citet{fang_homeowner_2023} find that Toronto councillors representing more homeowners are more likely to oppose housing, especially large projects within their own wards.
These papers are closest in spirit to the present project because they treat political geography as part of the housing supply technology.

The New York City setting adds a distinct form of variation.
The institutional change was not a town quietly revising its electoral rules or redrawing a ward boundary.
It was a court-induced charter reform that replaced the Board of Estimate with a district-based City Council at the final stage of land-use review.
Legal scholarship on New York land use has long argued that district-specific, seriatim review can lead city officials to ignore the aggregate housing costs of local restrictions \citep{hills_jr_balancing_2011,schleicher_city_2013}. \citet{been_urban_2014} also show that New York City rezonings often reflect homeowner and neighborhood politics rather than a simple growth-machine story.
This paper connects those institutional accounts to a testable prediction: after final authority shifts to a district-based council operating under member deference, large-building construction should fall most sharply in community districts where homeowner opposition was strongest.
