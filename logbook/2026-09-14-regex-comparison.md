---
title: "What our CPC regex shares with Tyler's approach"
date: "September 14, 2026"
author: "Codex research record"
---

Our existing regex and Tyler's described approach have the same basic architecture: isolate the relevant actor's section, recognize numbers and position language, and interpret whether a tally concerns approval or disapproval. Our parser already reverses disapproval-motion tallies. The main difference is what each approach attempts to measure. Tyler concentrates on formal community-board positions, votes, abstentions, and CPC hearing participation. Ours also attempts to identify substantive local requests, revisions or concessions, procedural responses, and responses to local concerns.

This comparison uses our code at checkpoint `90ad35b`, its current outputs, and Tyler's August 28, 2026 PDF supplied by Jacob. The extraction files named on page 4 of that PDF were not found in the local checkout or fetched Git history. The latest `origin/main` contains Tyler's hand-coding materials, but those are not his regex output. Therefore this is a code-to-description comparison with reproducible checks of our parser, not a measured agreement rate between two implementations.

Tyler's described output is more informative about how a number was obtained. It preserves the tally as printed, the proposal-aligned tally, abstentions, the formal board position, the rule that fired, and an evidence excerpt (pages 11–14). Our output preserves source and narrative hashes and companion applications, but its count columns omit the raw tally, abstentions, per-count evidence, and reason for a missing value. His six position categories distinguish approval with conditions, opposition with conditions, no recommendation, and no detected position. Our board-opposition measure instead uses a comparison of the two vote counts whenever both exist.

That definition produces a consequential difference in South Avenue Retail Development, C 160174 ZSR. Both approaches give 17 votes for and 14 against. The report also records five abstentions and says, "The abstentions are counted as disapproval votes." The board denied the recommendation to approve. Tyler reports the formal position as opposition; our exported `cb_opposition` is zero because 14 is less than 17. For analysis, formal recommendation, affirmative and negative votes, abstentions, and the rule governing the motion should remain distinct variables. This is stronger evidence than merely noting that two algorithms might choose different keywords.

Tyler also describes targeted repairs for spaced OCR words and a wider number vocabulary (pages 5–6). Our counting layer collapses whitespace and handles number words through sixty; it does not apply his spaced-word repairs. His speaker cascade preserves partial counts when only one side is reported (page 13). Ours can fill the other side with zero simply because the hearing was closed. Our vote extractor sums nonoverlapping matches, so repeated descriptions of one vote can be counted twice. The attached checks demonstrate these mechanisms on synthetic snippets; they do not estimate how often these errors occur in real reports. Conversely, our companion-report linkage and narrative deduplication address evidence spread across related applications, a feature not described in Tyler's note.

The available accuracy figures do not rank the two methods fairly. Tyler reports exact speaker agreement of 96.3% for supporting speakers and 99.2% for opposing speakers on 136 and 130 matched hand-coded observations, respectively (page 16). His note explicitly says the board tallies and their inversion had no direct hand-coded benchmark (pages 15 and 27). Our existing holdout directly includes board tallies, but its 100 ZM/ZR/ZS reports were labeled by Codex, not independently adjudicated by humans. It also counts unparsed values as misses and reports a second rate conditional on parsing. On this benchmark, broader context measures remain weak: revision or concession agrees on 65 of 100 reports and catches 31 of 64 reference-positive cases. These outcomes support testing LLM reading for context; they do not establish that an LLM is already accurate enough for the final data.

Coverage needs the same care. Our current output contains selected, deduplicated analysis narratives, while Tyler describes usable reports. The denominators and document bundles differ. His PDF also contains figures that need reconciliation against the generating output: page 14 reports 4,816 usable aligned tallies, about 43.1% of 11,188 reports, while page 19's table reports 63% board-vote coverage. Page 20 reports 4,481 observations in its six groups, whereas the group counts printed in the following figure sum to 5,397. These discrepancies could reflect different definitions or stale slides; neither interpretation is established here. They are reasons to compare the actual versioned outputs before interpreting coverage or trends.

My recommendation is to combine the useful parts around a shared extraction schema. Preserve literal statements and counts separately from interpretations such as "substantive concession" or "opposition unresolved at approval." Use regex for explicit counts and recognizable procedural statements, then test LLM reading where information spans sentences, reports, or revisions. Every extracted event should identify the project, application, document, actor, date, evidence location, and extraction method. Multiple votes and continued hearings need separate event rows before any aggregation. Keep "no recommendation," "not stated," "no source document," and "parser failed" separate from zero.

The next comparison should run both parsers on exactly the same source text, retain each parser's native outputs, and align only equivalent fields. Use a common human-adjudicated benchmark with older OCR, ordinary and inverted motions, abstentions, conditions, multiple hearings, and parser failures. Include a random sample of agreements as well as disagreements: both methods can be wrong together. Evaluate coverage, exact counts, false positives, and missed positives by period and action type; set an acceptable error rate for each research variable before using it in analysis. Testing cost and LLM escalation thresholds can follow that benchmark. This is a recommendation, not a methodological decision already approved by Jacob.

Finally, CPC extraction remains conditional on a report existing. The full ZAP project universe recovered earlier must supply the denominator for completion, withdrawal, and termination analyses. A withdrawn project without a CPC report should retain a source-availability flag, rather than receiving zero opposition or zero concessions. Neither parser can recover evidence that is absent from its source corpus.
